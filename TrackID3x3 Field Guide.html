Lesson 01

The challenge in one picture

What goes in, what must come out, and how it is judged.

A phone camera on a stand films a 3x3 basketball game from one fixed spot. Two teams of three wear coloured bibs with numbers. The organisers give us that video and ask a simple question for every single frame: where is each player standing, and who is it?

"Where" means a position on the court in centimetres, like a map coordinate. "Who" means the team (A or B) plus the jersey number. Team A is the team attacking at the very start of the video.

The answer is a plain text file with one line per player per frame:

frame_id,court_x,court_y,team_id,jersey_number
1,143.39,572.49,A,4
1,747.61,1049.83,A,10
1,938.18,494.67,A,12
… one line for each player, for every frame of the video
Key idea

A video is just a very long list of still pictures. The whole task is to turn each picture into six short lines of text.

Lesson 02

A video is a stack of number grids

Pixels, frames, frame rate, and why counting frames is harder than it sounds.

A digital picture is a grid of tiny coloured squares called pixels. Each pixel is three numbers: how much red, green and blue light it has. Our training videos are 3840 × 2160 pixels ("4K"), about 8.3 million pixels per picture.

A video shows about 30 pictures every second. Each picture is a frame. A 6½-minute game is therefore around 11,000 frames, and each needs its own answer.

Video setSize (pixels)Frames per videoUse
Tutorial clip IMG_0104_13840 × 21601,118Quick tests (37 seconds)
Training IMG_0104–01113840 × 216010,706–13,228Learning and validation
Challenge IMG_0114, 01151280 × 72011,536 / 11,823The final exam

Notice the last row: the challenge videos have 9 times fewer pixels. A jersey number that is 30 pixels tall in training is about 10 pixels tall in the challenge. That gap between practice and exam is called domain shift, and it matters a lot later.

Counting frames is a trap

A video file has a small header that says how many frames it contains. You would expect it to be correct. For IMG_0104 the header says 11,400, but when we actually decode every frame there are only 11,376, exactly where the official answers end. Phones also record with a slightly uneven frame rate (variable frame rate), so you cannot compute "frame number = time × 30".

Key idea

Always number frames by reading them one by one from the start: 1, 2, 3, … If our frame 500 is not the same moment as the answer key's frame 500, every comparison is wrong and the score collapses for no visible reason.

Lesson 03

Finding people: detection

A neural network draws boxes around everything that looks like a person.

Object detection means: given a picture, draw a rectangle around each object of interest and say what it is. The rectangle is a bounding box, stored as four numbers: the top-left corner (x, y), the width and the height, all in pixels.

We use YOLO11x ("You Only Look Once"), a neural network that has already learned from a huge public photo collection called COCO. It knows 80 kinds of object; we only ask for class 0, person. Each box comes with a confidence from 0 to 1: how sure the network is.

Key idea

We did not train YOLO ourselves. Using a pretrained public model is allowed by the rules, as long as we write down which one we used.

Lesson 04

Following people: tracking

Linking boxes across frames, and the mistakes that cost the most.

Detection works on one frame at a time. It has no idea that the box in frame 10 is the same person as a box in frame 11. Tracking links them: each person gets a track ID, a number that should stay with them.

We use ByteTrack. It predicts where each person will be next (they move smoothly) and pairs new boxes with existing tracks by how much they overlap. A continuous stretch of one ID is a tracklet.

Key idea

A swap is worse than losing someone for a moment. A gap costs a few frames; a swap makes every later frame of two players wrong. The dataset's authors call this the biggest single problem.

Tracks also break: a player is hidden for a second, comes back, and gets a new ID. On the 37-second tutorial clip the tracker made 38 tracks for 6 players plus bystanders. Joining the pieces back together is solved later by reading the jersey numbers.

Lesson 05

From pixels to centimetres: the homography

How four corner points let us turn a slanted photo into a flat map.

The camera looks at the court at an angle, so the rectangular court appears as a slanted shape (a trapezoid). The answer must be in court centimetres, as if looking straight down. The mathematical tool that converts one to the other is a homography: a 3 × 3 table of numbers that maps any point on the flat floor in the image to the same point on the map.

To compute it you need just four points whose position you know in both worlds. We use the four court corners. The organisers provide them in court_registration.csv:

CornerImage x (px)Image y (px)Court x (cm)Court y (cm)
RKU02698.81172.400
RKU13749.91726.601105
RKU10875.71237.915050
RKU11108.41970.315051105

Which point of a player do we map?

The homography is only correct for points on the floor. A player's head is not on the floor. So we take the foot point: the middle of the bottom edge of their box. When a player jumps, their feet leave the floor and the position briefly jumps too.

More landmarks for checking

Four corners define the homography exactly but give no way to check it. We built a court template with 11 known points (corners, paint corners, free-throw corners, the three-point line ends and the top of the arc), measured from the organisers' own court drawing at 1 pixel = 1 cm. Projecting them into a frame and seeing them land on the painted lines proves the calibration is right.

Lesson 06

Who is a player? The court filter

Simple rules on the court map remove referees, benches and passers-by.

Once every box has a court position, rules can decide who is playing:

  • Never on the court for 10 frames in a row → not a player.
  • More than half its time in the end-line band (3 m outside to 1 m inside) → probably a referee.
  • More than half its time in the far corner zones → probably a bystander.
  • Any single box more than 3 m outside the court → dropped.

On the tutorial clip this kept 23 of 38 tracks. We rendered a check video: grey boxes for everything the detector found, coloured boxes for what survived. Only bib-wearing players got coloured boxes.

Watch out

These rules were designed for the training camera. The challenge camera stands in a different corner, and the answer key shows real players up to 6 m outside the lines. The 3 m margin may need tuning.

Lesson 07

Who is who? Numbers, colours and rosters

Reading jersey numbers, splitting teams, and a fix that gained 0.12.

Reading the number: OCR

OCR (optical character recognition) reads text in images. We use EasyOCR, told to look only for digits. For each tracklet we pick up to 40 frames, enlarge the player crop 2×, read it, and vote: each reading adds its confidence to that number's score, and the highest total wins. One bad reading cannot outvote many good ones.

Splitting the teams: colour

We cut out each player's upper body and measure its colours as a histogram in HSV space (hue = which colour, saturation = how vivid). Then k-means sorts all tracklets into 2 groups by colour. The groups have no names yet.

Naming the teams: A or B

Team A is defined as the team attacking at the start. In frame 1, the player closest to the middle of the top line is taken as the attacker, so their colour group becomes A. The closest player to them is the defender, and that group becomes B.

The roster

Each game has a known roster: three numbers per team. In the tutorial: A = {4, 10, 12}, B = {6, 9, 11}. Team + number is a player's identity, for example A10. Two teams may share a number (in IMG_0108 both teams have a 6 and a 9), so the number alone is never enough.

Case study: the missing player

On the laptop, player B11 vanished from the whole clip. Comparing tracks with the answer key showed why: the colour grouping put B11's tracks in team B correctly, but OCR misread "11" as "4" or "12". Those numbers do not exist in team B, so the tracks were thrown away.

The fix (resolve_jerseys): trust the colour, treat an impossible number as a misread, and if a team is missing exactly one number, give its unread tracks that number. Score: 0.617 → 0.741. Our first attempt, trusting the number over the colour, made the score worse (0.524). Always measure.

Lesson 08

Filling the gaps and writing the answer

Joining tracklets, interpolating, and why every frame needs six rows.

After identification, several tracklets share one identity (B11 was made of 7 pieces). We merge them: for each frame and identity we keep the most confident box. Where a player is briefly missing, linear interpolation draws a straight line between the last position before the gap and the first after it.

Finally we write the file. The rules say every frame is scored, including before the game starts. A frame where we output only 5 players guarantees a missed player.

Planned improvement

Always output every known player in every frame, holding their first or last known position at the ends. Because a prediction only ever matches the same identity, an added row can turn a miss into a hit but can never cost more than the miss it replaces. A change that cannot lower the score is rare.

Lesson 09

Keeping score: TI-HOTA

How close is close enough, and why a wrong name scores zero.

The organisers have the true answer file, the ground truth. The score, TI-HOTA, compares our rows with it frame by frame. Two similarities are multiplied:

Location similarity: 1 when we are exactly on the spot, falling as the distance grows. The formula is exp(ln 0.05 × d² / τ²) with τ = 100 cm.
Identity similarity: 1 if team and number both match, otherwise 0.

Counting hits and misses

In each frame, predictions are paired with true players by the Hungarian method, which finds the best one-to-one pairing. Then every row falls into one of three bins:

DetA (detection accuracy) = hits ÷ (hits + wrong guesses + misses). AssA (association accuracy) asks, for each true player, how consistently they got the right name over time, then averages over the six players. TI-HOTA is the square root of DetA × AssA, averaged over 19 strictness levels (α from 0.05 to 0.95). At the strictest level a match must be within about 13 cm.

Key idea

AssA is averaged equally over the six players, as the organisers confirmed. A player you never find scores 0 for one sixth of AssA. That is why the missing B11 was so expensive. We checked our scorer against the official notebook: identical to six decimals.

Lesson 10

Building it properly

From a notebook to a project anyone can rerun and trust.

Modules, not one long notebook

The tutorial was one long Colab notebook. We split it into seven Python files, one per stage, then made them an installable package (pip install -e .) so import trackid3x3 works the same on a laptop and on Colab.

Tests

Small automatic checks prove the scorer is right: a perfect answer scores 1.0, an empty one 0, swapped numbers lower AssA, a 150 cm shift scores 0. Eleven tests now run in seconds with pytest.

Git: a time machine for code

Every change is a commit with a message. Push sends commits to GitHub, where Colab downloads them. A tag (baseline-v0) names one exact version so we can always return to it.

Laptop and Colab

Colab gives a free cloud GPU (Tesla T4); the laptop has an RTX 4050. The runner notebook clones the code, installs it, links the data on Google Drive, and runs the same commands as the laptop.

Surprise: two GPUs, two scores

The identical code scored 0.635 on Colab's T4 and 0.617 on the laptop. Different chips round numbers very slightly differently, which flipped a borderline OCR reading of B11. Lesson: compare versions only on the same machine, and build the method so one shaky reading cannot swing the result.

Lesson 11

Data and rules: practice versus exam

What we may learn from, what we may only run on, and how to test honestly.

Training set (8 games)

  • Videos with full answers: boxes, positions, teams, numbers
  • Rosters and court calibration
  • The only data we may train or tune on

Challenge set (2 games)

  • Videos, calibration and rosters, answers hidden
  • Run once, submit once, by 3 November
  • No training on them, no hand corrections

Testing without the exam answers

Since the challenge answers are hidden, we test on training games the model did not learn from: train on six, test on two, rotate. This is leave-videos-out validation. Testing on a game you trained on gives a flattering number, because the model has partly memorised it (overfitting).

What the data audit found

  • Every training video has boxes and answers from frame 1, with no gaps.
  • IMG_0104's header claims 24 more frames than it contains.
  • All 8 training videos have a variable frame rate.
  • IMG_0105 has 25 frames where one player appears twice.
  • Some frames in 0104, 0106 and 0111 have only 5 players.
